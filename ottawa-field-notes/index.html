Policy reality check · Cloud is already permitted

Existing federal direction: Treasury Board's Guideline on Service and Digital describes a cloud-first approach, including public cloud as the primary model to consider for Protected B or lower systems and an enterprise architecture review for alternatives. The November 2025 federal Digital Sovereignty Framework expressly recognizes legal, operational, security and supply-chain dimensions beyond geographical hosting.

Important qualification: Foreign legal process, concentration risk, classified data, export controls, availability and operational isolation are real issues. The appropriate comparison is control-by-control and workload-by-workload; on-premises infrastructure is sometimes justified.

“The question is not whether a supplier is American. It's whether the particular design meets Canada's documented controls—and whether the alternative actually performs better.”

This distinguishes an experience of unnecessarily restrictive implementation from a claim that Canada's published policy bans foreign technology.

NATO · procurement and Canadian export opportunity

Modernization delivered from Canada

An allied country's contribution should translate into a genuinely accessible route for its qualified firms to compete—regardless of where their engineers sit, when the work can securely be performed remotely.

6.5845%Canada's share of NATO common funding, 2026–27; not a reserved procurement entitlement
NATO RESTRICTEDApproved AWS capabilities in NATO-member-country Regions; further solution and personnel requirements still apply

Core question

“Canada contributes about 6.6% of NATO's common-funded budgets. Are Canadian technology companies receiving a fair opportunity to win NATO modernization work—including projects that can be securely delivered from Canada rather than requiring a presence in Brussels? Could Canada ask NATO to disclose its contract awards by supplier country, SME participation and delivery location, and examine whether an industrial-return or remote-delivery access initiative is needed?”

Use Canada's contribution as a benchmark for scrutiny, not an assertion that 6.6% of contracts is legally owed. NATO budgets include spending that is not addressable commercial procurement.

Short conversational version
“If Canadian firms can build and secure systems to NATO standards, why should digital modernization contracts require teams in Brussels when the work can be done just as securely from Canada? Are we measuring whether our industry is getting fair access to the work our country helps fund?”
If the discussion turns to a carve-out
“Rather than assume we need a protected national quota, could we first publish NATO awards and bid participation by member nation, benchmark Canada against its 6.6% contribution, and test an SME programme or industrial-return mechanism for eligible remote-deliverable digital work?”
What the official rules already permit
  • Eligibility: NATO's international competitive bidding generally admits firms from the NATO nations funding a common-funded requirement; an eligible Canadian firm can compete, subject to each solicitation and a national Declaration of Eligibility.
  • Delivery geography: Canada's NATO procurement guide quotes requirements allowing work, design, labour and services by firms from and within participating countries. Canada is one; NATO-wide rules do not universally require physical work in Brussels. Check each current solicitation for security, worksite and staffing constraints.
  • Cloud: On 22 September 2026 AWS announced approved cloud capabilities for NATO RESTRICTED data in approved AWS Regions within NATO nations. The security baseline helps allied accreditation, but doesn't automatically approve every workload, contract or supplier's access to NATO data.
  • Industrial return: NSPA has a production-balancing and industrial-return mechanism for NSPO member nations. This is not a uniform 6.6% Canadian procurement quota and does not automatically govern NCIA contracts.
  • Clearances: Required NATO security screening and document safeguarding still depend on the procurement; remote delivery is not a substitute for compliance.
Concrete follow-up to request

Ask for a Canadian NATO digital-procurement access report: total bid notices; Canadian Declarations of Eligibility and bids; shortlisted firms; awards and contract value; SME share; percentage of services performed in Canada; rates and reasons for mandatory in-country/onsite execution; clearance and procurement lead times. Separate NCIA and NSPA rather than pooling incompatible rules.

Potential implementation: test a technology-neutral, remotely deliverable NATO modernization procurement stream or SME access pilot. Preserve site attendance and classification restrictions only where tied to actual operational requirements. Ask whether a Canadian industrial-return objective is applicable or would require a formal NATO policy proposal.

A measured return of opportunity—competitive eligibility, fair evaluation, qualified remote delivery and transparent outcomes—is distinct from guaranteeing awards proportional to membership dues.

Category map

Six threads to pull

Each theme names a problem, its public relevance and a useful question. These are not descriptions of named commercial work.

01 · Legacy modernization

Frame: Updating critical systems begins with application ownership, dependencies and a realistic path away from fragile technology.

Consequence Reliability, change speed, cost and security all depend on understanding the existing estate.

“What should be measured: fewer failures, faster changes, reduced cost, or better service?”

Keep projected savings separate from realized results.

02 · Network visibility & cyber

Frame: Accurate IP address, subnet, asset and dependency inventories are basic enablers for security and modernization.

Consequence Unknown infrastructure creates avoidable incident-response and recovery blind spots.

“When something changes or fails, how quickly can teams establish what is affected?”
03 · Resilient compute & tactical communications

Frame: Some mission systems must operate despite limited, intermittent or unavailable connectivity.

Consequence Local processing, resilient identity and graceful synchronization can be as important as the backhaul.

“Which functions must continue when reach-back is unavailable, and for how long?”

Distinguish architectures, demonstrations and operationally fielded systems.

04 · Uncrewed air & maritime systems

Frame: Sensors, safe mission planning, assured communication, edge inference and autonomy need to work as a coordinated system.

Consequence Civilian and defence platforms can share technical requirements without sharing operational constraints.

“Where is the limiting factor: sensing, data processing, coordination, connectivity, or integration?”

A procurement qualification is eligibility to compete, not a contract. Official DDI background ↗

05 · Procurement & commercialization

Frame: Research funding, qualified supplier lists, pilots, purchase orders and field adoption are separate stages.

Consequence Strong technology does not automatically translate into timely operational procurement.

“Where does the path stall between an identified requirement and a funded deployment?”

Seek efficiency without assuming competition, security reviews or oversight can be skipped.

06 · Sovereignty, privacy & responsible AI

Frame: Data residency, legal jurisdiction, access, encryption-key ownership and service resilience are related but not interchangeable.

Consequence The correct hosting model depends on classification, obligations and operational requirements.

“Who can access the data, who controls keys, and what continues to work if the provider is unavailable?”
